Designer’s guide Schell-inspired questions and experiments

Use these questions to investigate your puzzle. In Jesse Schell’s The Art of Game Design, third edition, puzzles are in Chapter 14, with Lens 58: The Puzzle. Chapter 13 concerns balance. These are original classroom applications of his principles, not excerpts from the book.

Start with a prediction.

“If I change ___, I expect a player to ___ because ___.” Name something you could observe that would make you question your prediction. Change one thing before adding more.

01 · A CLEAR GOAL

Can the player tell what success means?

Try: revise the exit marker or the opening instruction.

Watch: ask a new player what they are trying to accomplish before their first move.

02 · AN ACCESSIBLE START

What can one move teach?

Try: a tiny introductory board that demonstrates sliding and stopping.

Watch: can the player predict where the next move will end without your explanation?

03 · VISIBLE PROGRESS

What has the player learned so far?

Try: mark visited stopping positions as an exploration record.

Watch: does that record inform a decision? Being nearer the door does not necessarily mean being nearer a solution.

04 · CONFIDENCE IN A SOLUTION

Why believe a route exists?

Try: a solver-verified assurance or a clue about a reachable stopping point.

Watch: when does the player stop guessing? Distinguish solvability from the start from solvability at the current position.

05 · A LEARNING SEQUENCE

What prepares the player for the next board?

Try: three boards that introduce, vary, and combine an idea.

Watch: can the player use that idea on a new board? A longer shortest path alone does not prove greater difficulty.

06 · PARALLEL CHALLENGES

What else could someone try when stuck?

Try: offer a choice of two puzzles with a way to return.

Watch: does switching help the player discover an idea, or leave them unsure where to begin?

07 · COMBINING IDEAS

Can two small discoveries solve a larger puzzle?

Try: two short teaching boards and a third requiring both ideas.

Watch: ask which earlier discovery helped. Look for transfer rather than repeated trial and error.

08 · USEFUL HINTS

What is the smallest helpful nudge?

Try: an optional question, then a relevant feature, then the next move.

Watch: record the hint level requested. Can the player explain their next decision afterward?

09 · LEARNING FROM AN ANSWER

What could revealing a solution teach?

Try: a clearly labeled, optional walkthrough followed by a new board using the same idea.

Watch: can the player apply what they saw? Keep a hint distinct from a full reveal.

10 · A CHANGE IN PERSPECTIVE

When does a surprising insight feel fair?

Try: a board whose solution begins by moving away from the exit.

Watch: what assumption changes? Ask whether the discovery felt satisfying or arbitrary, and why.

Watch someone play

  1. Give a neighbor the game and its on-screen instructions. Observe without coaching.
  2. Record the board, version, order, hints, resets, completion, and one revealing action or remark. Use anonymous labels.
  3. Ask “What were you trying to do?” and “What changed your understanding?”
  4. Compare the observation with your prediction, note a limitation, and revise one thing.

Keep the evidence honest

A faster second solve may reflect remembering the solution. Use different players or alternate the order across pairs; use comparable new boards to examine transfer.

Rule tests establish specified behavior. The solver establishes reachability. Human play and conversation provide evidence about understanding and experience. A tiny classroom sample remains exploratory.

Use the editor to try original boards, Check & play to verify reachability, and Exchange a board to trade with a partner. The move trace records actions, not feelings.

Codex starter guide From your first prompt to a reviewable variation

Use Codex as your building assistant. You choose the design question, inspect the change, and interpret the human playtest. This browser game itself needs no account or model connection.

  1. Open the right project

    Install the course’s Codex CLI or native app and sign in through the announced account setup. Open your checkout of columbia-university-ai-games/game-dev-f26. In its terminal, check git status, git remote -v, python3 --version, and node --version. Use Python 3.11+ and Node 22+; Windows may use py instead of python3.

    Preserve existing work before switching branches. A downloaded game or gallery is enough to play, but the commands below need the full repository checkout.

  2. Find the course skill

    Ask Codex to read AGENTS.md. Find minigame-variation through /skills in the CLI and invoke $minigame-variation. If it is missing, restart Codex in the updated checkout or explicitly ask it to read .agents/skills/minigame-variation/SKILL.md.

    The skill supplies the repeatable workflow; your prompt supplies today’s idea. No third-party plugin or connector is required. Inspect any optional plugin’s capabilities and account permissions before connecting it.

  3. Create your own copy

    From an updated, clean main, run the example below at the repository root. Replace YOUR-LOGIN with your lowercase GitHub login. hints is one exercise choice.

    git switch -c YOUR-LOGIN/get-out-hints
python3 scripts/minigames.py new YOUR-LOGIN --principle hints

    Edit only minigames/submissions/YOUR-LOGIN/get-out/. If that copy already exists, resume its folder and branch. The scaffold refuses to overwrite it.

  4. Give Codex a bounded task

    Write your prediction first, then adapt this prompt:

    $minigame-variation My login is YOUR-LOGIN and my principle is hints. My prediction is: [my words]. Inspect my existing copy and experiment.md. Propose one small change with affected files and checks, then implement the change I select. Keep edits inside my game folder. Run relevant tests and explain one changed function. Leave human playtest evidence for me to supply.

    Ask for clarification when an explanation does not match the code. Keep the change small enough to playtest today.

  5. Check the behavior and watch a person

    python3 scripts/minigames.py check --owner YOUR-LOGIN
python3 scripts/minigames.py test --owner YOUR-LOGIN

    Open your copy’s index.html. Check the changed interaction, keyboard/touch controls, undo, reset, hints, and a win. Rule changes need corresponding tests and a consistent hint solver.

    Trade games using the designer’s guide above. Record actual observations in experiment.md. You write the prediction, interpretation, and AI disclosure; missing human evidence remains TODO.

  6. Review, then prepare one draft PR

    Use a separate Codex review task: “Review my game folder against my prediction and tests. Find concrete bugs, misleading feedback, and unsupported claims. Report file references and checks actually run. Do not edit or invent playtest evidence.” Resolve useful findings and inspect the diff.

    python3 scripts/minigames.py check --owner YOUR-LOGIN --ready
python3 scripts/minigames.py gallery

    Run --ready after completing the report. Open build/minigames/index.html to find your card; leave generated files uncommitted. Stage only your own game folder, commit, push your branch to your class-repo remote or fork, and open one draft PR into the class repo’s main.
